How this was made

All 3,244 games were downloaded through the Lichess and chess.com public APIs into a local SQLite database. Every rated blitz and rapid game (2,818 of them) was then replayed and evaluated move by move: with Lichess's own server analysis where it existed, otherwise with Stockfish 17 at depth 14 running locally. A blunder is a move that worsens the evaluation by two pawns or more, and each one is classified by what the engine's best reply would have punished. Clock figures come from the per-move clock times stored in the game records. The tables below are computed straight from that database. The reading of the last twenty games, the verdict and the plan were written by an AI coaching assistant working from those games and that analysis; they are opinions, to be tested over the next eight weeks.

The pattern

  1. You do not look at what the opponent's move does. Threats to your king, to your queen, to a piece you just moved, to the pawn behind the piece you just moved. Games 5, 8, 14, 15, 17, 19, 20 above were each decided by one such move.
  2. You do not convert. Won positions are lost to the clock, to a missed passed pawn, or because a basic mate is missing.
  3. Your king is an afterthought. Castling into an attack, losing castling rights, letting a queen sit next to the king.
  4. Your black repertoire is five different openings, none of which you know. Sicilian (43%), Nimzowitsch 1...Nc6 (46%), French, Caro-Kann, Benoni, Philidor. Against the London and Italian you score around 35%. As white the Queen's Gambit gives you decent positions and results around 50%, which is fine.
  5. Early queen wandering. Qf6 on move 3, Qa5 then Qf5, bishops that move three times. Tempi go missing and the middlegame starts with your pieces in the wrong places.

What I want you to do for the next eight weeks

You said 15 hours a week. Here is how I would spend them. The goal is rapid 1138 to 1300 by early November, then 1400. Blitz will follow on its own.

  1. Play rapid, not blitz. 10+5 or 15+10. At most three rated games in a sitting, about 6 hours a week. Blitz is a reward, one or two games, after a rapid session. This is the rule I care most about.
  2. Before every move, one question: "what does his last move attack, and what will my move leave hanging?" Checks, captures, forks, the square I am leaving. Say it in your head. This is a 3-second habit and it is worth 200 rating points on its own. In the 10+5 games you have the time; the data says you are not using it.
  3. Basic mates until they are automatic. King and queen, king and rook, two rooks, then queen against rook, then king and pawn versus king. Fifteen minutes a day on the Lichess practice section for two weeks. You will never again draw with three pieces against a bare king.
  4. Simplify black. Drop the Sicilian and 1...Nc6. Against 1.e4 play 1...e5 and learn to reach a safe, developed position against the Italian, the Ruy Lopez and the Scotch by move 10. Against 1.d4 play 1...d5 and the Queen's Gambit Declined, which you already understand from the white side. That is two openings instead of six, and they teach the classical principles you are missing. I will write you a one-page sheet for each.
  5. Review every loss for five minutes. Find the one move. Not the whole game, the one move. I will send you a weekly page of positions from your own games where you went wrong, as puzzles, with your move and the right one. That is the drill that no site provides and the one thing worth building here.
  6. Keep the puzzles, change the flavour. Fifteen minutes a day. Use Lichess puzzle themes: hanging piece, fork, discovered attack, mate in one, defensive move. Your puzzle rating is already good; the point is to train the "is something hanging" reflex, not calculation depth.
  7. Do not resign before move 30 unless you are down a full piece with no play. Two of your last ten blitz losses were resignations with plenty of time in positions that were equal or a pawn down.

Roughly: 6 hours playing, 2 hours endgames and openings, 2 hours puzzles, 2 hours reviewing losses and drilling your own mistakes. That is 12; the rest is slack.
